1  Dataset descriptions

1.1 Sea surface temperature (SST)

Sea surface temperature (SST) data come from NOAA’s daily Optimum Interpolation Sea Surface Temperature (OISST) version 2.1 (Huang et al. (2021)). We download the final (not preliminary) daily files from NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) archive, and check that each file holds the expected date before using it. The raw data are provided globally at 0.25x0.25 degree daily resolution. We keep the pixels within the geographic analysis scope (see below), and temporally aggregate the daily data to monthly resolution by calculating the mean (and standard deviation, minimum, and maximum) SST for each pixel from across the days in each month. Every day from 2016-01-01 through 2025-12-31 is included.

1.2 Oceanic Niño Index (ONI)

Monthly Oceanic Niño Index (ONI) values come from NOAA’s Physical Sciences Laboratory. The ONI is NOAA’s primary index of the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO): the 3-month running mean of sea surface temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region (5°N-5°S, 120°-170°W), from NOAA’s Extended Reconstructed SST (ERSST) dataset. Values of +0.5°C or higher indicate El Niño conditions, and -0.5°C or lower indicate La Niña conditions. NOAA revises past ONI values when it updates ERSST or the index’s base periods, so values for earlier months can shift slightly between data pulls. To keep the analysis reproducible, we use a copy of the ONI file saved on 2026-11-02, and use the ONI for each month from 2016-01-01 through 2025-12-31.

1.3 Sea surface temperature (SST) forecasts under climate change

We use SST forecasts under climate change from the IPCC WGI Interactive Atlas(Iturbide et al. (2021)). The Atlas provides a platform for accessing ensemble forecasts from CMIP6 (Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6), which represent the latest global climate forecasts available and served as the basis of the 6th IPCC Assessment Report.

We use the data underlying the Atlas, the IPCC-WGI AR6 Interactive Atlas Dataset (Instituto de Física de Cantabria, Version 2 final), which has monthly SST from each CMIP6 model interpolated to a common 1x1 degree grid. We read it from the dataset’s THREDDS server, for the 1x1 degree cells within the geographic analysis scope. For each scenario and time horizon, we calculate each model’s mean SST for each calendar month across the years of the time horizon it has data for, then average across the models with SST in each cell. This follows the Atlas’s own code (datasets-interactive-atlas/04_map_figures.R in Iturbide et al. (2021)), and reproduces the values on the Interactive Atlas to within 0.02°C. A few model runs don’t cover every year (e.g., the CAMS-CSM1-0 and IITM-ESM runs end before 2100, and EC-Earth3-Veg-LR is listed for SSP5-8.5 but has no SST data, so SSP5-8.5 means use 26 models); the models and years used are listed in cmip6_models_used.csv in the pipeline outputs. We use three time horizons:

  1. Near Term (2021-2040)
  2. Medium Term (2041-2060)
  3. Long Term (2081-2100)

And for each time horizon, four climate change scenarios (more information on the scenarios can be found here), each with the models the Atlas uses for it:

  1. SSP1-2.6 (26 models)
  2. SSP2-4.5 (28 models)
  3. SSP3-7.0 (24 models)
  4. SSP5-8.5 (27 models)

This gives us a total of 12 different forecasts to analyze. The pipeline checks that each has all 12 months, and that no two are identical. (Until November 2026, we downloaded these forecasts by hand from the Interactive Atlas website. Some of those files turned out to be copies of a different scenario or time horizon, and the Near Term files had no February.) The dataset can be loaded in targets using targets::tar_load(sst_cc_forecast_data), and it has the following columns:

  • lon_bin: 1x1 degree longitude bin, lower-left corner (degrees) (numeric)
  • lat_bin: 1x1 degree latitude bin, lower-left corner (degrees) (numeric)
  • sst_deg_c_mean: Multi-model mean sea surface temperature for the calendar month (degrees C) (numeric)
  • time_period: Future time horizon forecast time period (character)
  • scenario: Climate change scenario (character)
  • month_number: Month number (numeric)

1.4 AIS-based fishing effort

We use satellite-based individual vessel monitoring AIS data processed by Global Fishing Watch (Kroodsma et al. (2018)). We use the V5 pipeline table global-fishing-watch.pipe_ais_v5_published.messages. Variables of interest within this table include the following (descriptions are taken directly from the schema for pipe_ais_v5_published.messages):

  • ssvid: source specific vessel id; MMSI for AIS
  • hours: time since the previous position in the segment
  • timestamp: timestamp for position
  • lon: longitude
  • lat: latitude
  • night_loitering: 1 if the seg_id of every message of a squid_jigger that is at night and not moving, 0 if not.

In order to minimize noisy data, we only include AIS messages that have a clean_segs boolean (i.e., all messages must have good_seg boolean and must not have an overlapping_and_short boolean). We filter to just those messages where night_loitering = 1. For squid jigging vessels, GFW uses the heuristic of night loitering to identify when they are fishing. Therefore, any hours where night_loitering = 1 can be classified as fishing_hours

We take the raw high-resolution AIS data and aggregate fishing_hours spatially (by 0.25x0.25 degree pixels, which are roughly 27.75km x 27.75km at the equator), temporally by month, and by flag. We currently process data from 2016-01-01 through 2025-12-31.

1.5 AIS-based vessel info

Vessel characteristics data processed are by Global Fishing Watch (Park et al. (2023)). We use the V5 pipeline table global-fishing-watch.pipe_ais_identity_v5_partners_published.vi_ssvid, which has one row per vessel summarizing its whole AIS record. In V5, vessel class, whether a vessel is a fishing vessel, and engine power come from GFW’s random forest vessel classification model (random_forest_info), which replaces the V3 fields best.best_vessel_class and on_fishing_list_best (GFW Pipe5 Release Notes for Research Partners, September 2026). Variables of interest within this table include the following (descriptions of the random_forest_info fields are from the release notes, since the schema does not describe them; the others are taken directly from the schema):

  • ssvid: source specific vessel id; MMSI for AIS
  • best.best_flag: best flag state (ISO3) for the vessel
  • random_forest_info.on_fishing_list_best: whether the vessel is a fishing vessel, from the random forest model, overridden by official registry information where available
  • random_forest_info.best_vessel_class: best vessel class for the vessel from the random forest model. The model only names a specific gear when it scores that gear at least 0.35; otherwise it rolls the vessel up to a broader class, such as the generic fishing
  • random_forest_info.atomic_class: the random forest model’s finest-level vessel class prediction
  • random_forest_info.best_engine_power_kw: best engine power (kilowatts) for the vessel (using official registry information where available, or the random forest model’s estimate where not available)
  • activity.active_hours: hours the vessel was broadcasting AIS and moving more than 0.1 knots
  • activity.offsetting: true if this vessel has been seen with an offset postition at some point between 2012 and 2019
  • activity.overlap_hours_multinames: the total numbers of hours of overlap between two segments where, over the time period of the two segments that overlap (including the non-overlapping time of the segments), the vessel broadcast two or more normalized name, where each normalized name was bradcast at least 10 or more times. That is a bit complicated, but the goal is to identify overlapping segments where there were likely more than one identity. (this should be 0; if it is > 0, it can be used as a filter to remove potentially erroneous/noisy vessels)

We filter to just those vessels the random forest model calls fishing vessels (random_forest_info.on_fishing_list_best) and squid jiggers. A vessel is a squid jigger when random_forest_info.best_vessel_class = squid_jigger, or when the model rolls the vessel up to the generic fishing class but its finest-level prediction is random_forest_info.atomic_class = squid_jigger (GFW says this finest-level prediction is reliable for squid jiggers). Additionally, to reduce noise, we filter out vessels that broadcast exceedingly infrequently (i.e., activity.active_hours < 24) or are noisy vessels, using GFW’s definition of a noisy vessel (i.e., activity.offsetting or activity.overlap_hours_multinames >= 24). They are simply not reliable and will not provide good effort estimates. This leaves us with 4,212 squid vessels globally.

1.5.1 Joined dataset: SST and AIS-based effort

One version of the final dataset we use for our analysis is a combination of the gridded AIS-based fishing effort data and gridded SST data. We start from every 0.25x0.25 degree pixel and month with SST data within the analysis scope, crossed with every flag that has fishing effort in the analysis scope. We then left join the AIS-based effort onto that grid by pixel, month, and flag, so pixel-months with no effort for a flag have zero effort. Each row in the joined dataset represents flag-level effort in a given pixel and month, with the corresponding SST for that pixel and month, the ONI for that month, and the pixel’s EEZ information.

The joined dataset can be loaded in R using the command targets::tar_load(joined_dataset_ais). The dataset has the following columns:

  • month: Month (first day of month) (date)
  • lon_bin: 0.25 degree longitude bin (degrees) (numeric)
  • lat_bin: 0.25 degree latitude bin (degrees) (numeric)
  • flag: Fishing vessel flag (ISO3) (character)
  • sst_deg_c_mean: Mean sea surface temperature, aggregated from the raw daily 0.25x0.25 degree data (degrees C) (numeric). The dataset also has the standard deviation, minimum, and maximum (sst_deg_c_sd, sst_deg_c_min, sst_deg_c_max)
  • fishing_hours: Total fishing effort across vessels (hours) (numeric)
  • fishing_kw_hours: Total fishing effort across vessels (kW-hours) (numeric)
  • oceanic_nino_index: Oceanic Niño Index for the month (degrees C) (numeric)
  • eez_id, eez_iso3, high_seas: The Marine Regions v12 EEZ (MRGID) the pixel’s centroid falls in, and its sovereign (ISO3), or high_seas (Institute (2023)). Joint regimes and disputed areas are not counted as EEZs, so pixels in them are high_seas
  • nearest_eez_id, nearest_eez_iso3, nearest_eez_distance_m: The sovereign (Marine Regions sovereign MRGID and ISO3) of the pixel’s EEZ, or for high seas pixels, of the nearest EEZ, and the distance (meters) from the pixel’s centroid to the nearest 200 nautical mile boundary between an EEZ and the high seas

1.6 Geographic analysis scope

Our proposed geographic scope encompasses a bounding box with a longitude range from -130 degrees to -70 degrees and a latitude range from -40 degrees to 10 degrees (Figure 1.1; Figure 1.2). This longitude range encompasses band of equatorial fishing effort to the west and the EEZs off the western coast of South America. The latitude range covers the maximum latitude of the South Pacific Regional Fisheries Management Organisation (SPRFMO) and extends beyond the southern latitude where the north-south band of fishing effort is currently concentrated. The bounding box extends beyond where fishing effort is currently concentrated, which means that in our predictions under future climate change scenarios, we could capture shifts of fishing effort beyond it current range.
